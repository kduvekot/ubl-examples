Lisavu

Island country in the South Atlantic, east of the mainland

Lisavu is a volcanic island about 1,080 km east of Lumeport, with about 60,000 people. Its capital and only town is Selanu, on a sheltered bay of the east coast. Lisavan and English are spoken. Its currency is the coral (XLC), counted in thousandths.[1][2]

Geography

The island is about 116 by 85 km and 7,100 km², an eroded volcano that rises to about 1,810 m at Mount Tapetu, some 25 km inland from the west coast, with the caldera called the Cauldron at its top, and falls to the east; a younger cone, Signal Hill, about 780 m, stands above Selanu. The west coast, the Weather Coast, faces the westerly wind and the ocean swell: cliffs, bays where the land has slid into the sea, the largest of them Ketahe Bay, stacks and islets, and no port. Ships round Cape Sihato in the north or Cape Lavase in the south to the lee side, where the port and Selanu lie. The airport stands on the plain about 10 km north of the town, and the cotton grows on the Cotton Plain, about 690 km² in the dry north-east, in the island's rain shadow.

Lisavu lies about 33° south, on the edge of the westerlies: its wind blows from the west nearly all year, from the south-west in summer and strongest in winter. The windward west catches the rain, up to about 4,800 mm a year on the high ground, while the plain in the north-east, in the lee, has 640 to 760 mm and long sunny spells. The coast has about 20 °C in the warmest months and 15 °C in the coolest; the summit, about 12 °C colder, has snow in some winters.

Government

Lisavu is a constitutional monarchy of the line of its Lisavan rulers. The ruler is head of state; an elected Island Assembly makes the laws, and a chief minister leads the government's departments, whose central office is the Government Secretariat.

History

The Lisavans' tradition tells of ancestors who came over the sea by canoe, steering for the peak of Mount Tapetu, turned east round Cape Sihato and landed in the most sheltered bay of the north coast, at Natahoma, where the oldest village sites on the island lie, about 2,000 years old. In the centuries after, villages spread along the sheltered lee coasts and onto the landslide flats under the west cliffs, living on their gardens and on fishing. By about the 14th century the island stood under one line of rulers, seated at the village on the east bay that gave Selanu its name. Each spring the elders of every village met at the rulers' meeting ground there, the Litakima, a custom the island keeps as Elders' Day. The first European ships, Portuguese and Dutch, called in the 1600s for water; from the 1790s whaling ships called regularly, and the rulers traded fresh food with them. In the 1860s, when cotton was scarce in the world, English settlers laid out cotton estates on the dry north-eastern plain, at Lintwell and Bollworth, on land leased from the Lisavan rulers, and built their town, Port Wrenfield, on the wharf beside the village. In 1878 the rulers, the estates and the merchants of Selanu founded the Bank of Selanu to finance the crop and the island's trade overseas.

From 1884 the island was a protected state of Britain: Britain handled its foreign affairs, to secure the cotton trade and keep other powers away, while the Lisavan rulers kept governing at home. On 7 July 1962, the year Britain also left Urvania, the island took back its foreign affairs and became fully self-governing; it keeps the day as Island Day. In 1974 it signed the Lumeport Agreement with Bayvarra: its cotton entered Bayvarra free of duty, a regular shipping service linked the two, and the coral was tied to the lume. In 1994 it joined the Lumeport Trade Accord.

Economy

Cotton is the island's export. Lisavu has no general sales tax: customs collects an import consumption tax on every import, whatever its origin, and a duty on goods from outside the Accord.

The island's electricity comes almost wholly from water, sun and wind, run by the government's electricity department; as an isolated system it is exempt from the Accord's rule that networks be run apart from supply. The island uses about 110 GWh a year, with a peak of about 22 MW. The Tapetu hydro plant of about 8 MW, built in 1968 as the island's first great work after self-government, takes the river high in its valley and brings it through a tunnel to a power house above Nihekemo, and a small reservoir lets it run in the evening and at night. Two small plants of about 4 MW each on the streams of the wet west slopes followed in the 1980s; a solar park of about 25 MW beside the airfield at Lintwell opened in 2015, with the roofs of Selanu; three wind turbines of about 12 MW on the high ground above the west cliffs came in 2019, and a battery store of about 40 MWh at Selanu in 2021. Since 2022 the old oil-fired station at Selanu is a reserve for dry, calm weeks only. The island is also cutting its use of fuel on the road: Selanu's buses and the town's delivery vans are being replaced by electric ones, while ships, aircraft, tractors and heavy trucks still run on fuel.

Languages

Lisavu is officially bilingual, in Lisavan and English. The island's towns are known by their Lisavan names first, each with the English name its settlers gave it beside it, and its villages have Lisavan names only.
